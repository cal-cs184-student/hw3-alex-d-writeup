Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation

A ray is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d},\; 0 \le t < \infty\). To supersample each pixel, raytrace_pixel picks ns_aa random points inside the pixel, generates a ray through each, and averages the radiance they return, which is a Monte Carlo estimate of the pixel's value.

generate_ray maps a normalized image point \((x, y) \in [0,1]^2\) onto the sensor at \(z = -1\) in camera space:

\[ \mathbf{D}_{cam} = \left((2x-1)\tan\frac{hFov}{2},\; (2y-1)\tan\frac{vFov}{2},\; -1\right). \]

The ray starts at the camera position. I rotate \(\mathbf{D}_{cam}\) into world space with c2w and normalize it to get the direction.

Primitive intersection

Each ray is tested against every primitive. A hit counts only if \(t_{min} \le t \le t_{max}\), and each valid hit sets \(t_{max} \leftarrow t\), so later primitives must be closer to count. The closest hit is left at the end, and its \(t\), normal, primitive and BSDF are stored. For now, the pixel is colored by the normal.

For a sphere with center \(\mathbf{c}\) and radius \(R\), setting \(\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{r}(t)\) in \((\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})^2 - R^2 = 0\) gives a quadratic \(at^2 + bt + c = 0\) with \(a = \mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{d}\), \(b = 2(\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{c})\cdot\mathbf{d}\) and \(c = (\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{c})\cdot(\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{c}) - R^2\), so

\[ t = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}. \]

A negative discriminant means a miss. Otherwise I take the nearest root in range. The normal points from \(\mathbf{c}\) to the hit point.

Triangle intersection algorithm

I used the Möller-Trumbore algorithm. A hit point on triangle \(P_0P_1P_2\) can be written with barycentric weights as \(\mathbf{O} + t\mathbf{D} = (1-b_1-b_2)\mathbf{P}_0 + b_1\mathbf{P}_1 + b_2\mathbf{P}_2\). Solving these three equations for the three unknowns gives

\[ \left[\begin{array}{c} t \\ b_1 \\ b_2 \end{array}\right] = \frac{1}{\mathbf{S}_1 \cdot \mathbf{E}_1} \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{S}_2 \cdot \mathbf{E}_2 \\ \mathbf{S}_1 \cdot \mathbf{S} \\ \mathbf{S}_2 \cdot \mathbf{D} \end{array}\right] \]

with \(\mathbf{E}_1 = \mathbf{P}_1 - \mathbf{P}_0\), \(\mathbf{E}_2 = \mathbf{P}_2 - \mathbf{P}_0\), \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{O} - \mathbf{P}_0\), \(\mathbf{S}_1 = \mathbf{D} \times \mathbf{E}_2\) and \(\mathbf{S}_2 = \mathbf{S} \times \mathbf{E}_1\). The ray hits the triangle when \(b_1, b_2 \ge 0\), \(b_1 + b_2 \le 1\) and \(t\) is in range. A denominator near zero means the ray is parallel to the triangle, so it misses. I blend the three vertex normals with the same weights, which makes curved meshes look smooth.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH construction

The BVH is a binary tree where each node stores a bounding box around its primitives. construct_bvh builds it recursively. For a list of primitives, I first compute the bounding box of all of them and make a node with that box. If there are at most max_leaf_size primitives, the node becomes a leaf, and its start and end iterators point at that range of the primitive vector.

Otherwise I split the primitives into two groups. I pick the axis along which the primitives' bounding box centroids are most spread out, since splitting along the longest extent separates the geometry best. The split point is the average centroid along that axis. std::partition then reorders the primitives in place so that those with centroids below the split come first, and the two halves are built recursively as the left and right children. Because the partition happens in place, every leaf's iterators point at a contiguous range of the original vector, and no primitives are copied.

I chose the average centroid because it adapts to how the primitives are distributed. A midpoint split can leave one side nearly empty when a mesh has a dense region, while the average follows the dense region. If every centroid still lands on one side (for example, when they are all equal), recursing on the same list would never end. In that case I fall back to splitting at the median primitive with std::nth_element, which always puts half the primitives on each side.

Bounding box intersection

A box is the intersection of three slabs, one pair of axis-aligned planes per axis. For the plane \(x = p'_x\), the ray hits at \(t = \frac{p'_x - o_x}{d_x}\), which gives an entry and exit time \([t_{x,min}, t_{x,max}]\) for the x slab. The ray is inside the box where all three slab intervals overlap:

\[ t_{enter} = \max(t_{x,min}, t_{y,min}, t_{z,min}), \quad t_{exit} = \min(t_{x,max}, t_{y,max}, t_{z,max}). \]

I also clamp this interval to the ray's \([t_{min}, t_{max}]\). The ray hits the box if \(t_{enter} \le t_{exit}\), which also covers rays that start or end inside the box.

BVH intersection

Traversal starts at the root. If the ray misses a node's box, nothing inside it can be hit, so the whole subtree is skipped. At a leaf, the ray is tested against each primitive. At an interior node, I recurse into both children. intersect has to check both children even after one reports a hit, because the boxes can overlap and the other child might hold a closer surface. Since each primitive already shrinks \(t_{max}\) when it records a hit, the closest hit wins, and later box tests reject anything farther away. has_intersection only needs to know whether any hit exists, so it returns as soon as one child reports one.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Diffuse BSDF and zero-bounce light

A diffuse (Lambertian) surface scatters light equally in every direction, so its BSDF is a constant, \(f = \frac{\rho}{\pi}\), where \(\rho\) is the surface's reflectance. Zero-bounce light is light that goes straight from a light source to the camera, so zero_bounce_radiance just returns the emission of the surface the camera ray hits. That is why only the area light shows up when the max ray depth is 0.

Direct lighting

Direct lighting is the light that reaches a hit point \(p\) straight from a light source and then reflects toward the camera. It is given by the reflection equation, which I estimate with Monte Carlo:

\[ L_{out}(p, \omega_o) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_j \to \omega_o)\, L_{in}(p, \omega_j) \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)}. \]

Both of my direct lighting functions use this estimator. They differ only in how they choose the incoming directions \(\omega_j\). The BSDF is evaluated in the local frame at the hit point, where the normal is \(z\), so \(\cos\theta_j\) is just the \(z\) component of \(\omega_j\). Rays are cast in world space, so I convert directions with o2w. Every ray leaving a surface starts at \(t_{min} = \epsilon\) so it doesn't hit the surface it came from.

Uniform hemisphere sampling picks \(N\) directions uniformly over the hemisphere around the normal, so \(p(\omega_j) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\). For each direction, I cast a ray from \(p\). If it hits something, that surface's emission is \(L_{in}\), and anything that isn't a light has zero emission. Most of these rays miss the light, so they add nothing.

Light sampling (importance sampling) picks directions toward the lights instead. For each light, sample_L gives a direction toward a point on the light, its distance, its radiance and its pdf. I skip samples where the light is behind the surface (\(\cos\theta \le 0\)). Otherwise I cast a shadow ray toward the light with \(t_{max}\) = distance \(- \epsilon\). If nothing blocks it, the sample adds its term to the sum. Area lights get ns_area_light samples each. Point lights get one sample, since every sample of a point light is the same. Because every sample is aimed at a light, this also works for point lights, which uniform hemisphere sampling can never hit.

Uniform hemisphere vs. light sampling

Both use 64 samples per pixel and 32 light rays.

Light rays and soft shadows

These use CBbunny with 1 sample per pixel and light sampling, varying the number of light rays (-l).

With 1 light ray, each point in the soft shadow is either fully lit or fully dark, depending on whether its single shadow ray happened to reach the light. That makes the shadow under the bunny a speckle of black and white pixels. With more light rays, each point averages over more points on the area light, so it gets the right fraction of lit samples. At 16 rays the penumbra is a smooth gradient with light grain, and at 64 rays it is almost noise-free.

Analysis

With the same number of samples, light sampling is far less noisy than uniform hemisphere sampling. The area light covers only a small part of the hemisphere above each point, so most uniform samples miss it and add zero, while the few that hit add a large value. That gives a high-variance estimate, which shows up as grain over every surface in the hemisphere renders. Light sampling sends every sample toward the light, so a sample is wasted only when the point is actually in shadow. The result is smooth walls and clean soft shadows at the same sample count, and the light-sampled bunny even rendered faster (18 s vs. 28 s). Both methods converge to the same image, but light sampling gets there with far fewer samples. One visible difference is that the hemisphere renders show a glow around the light on the ceiling. Hemisphere sampling treats the light as emitting in every direction, while the light-sampling code models the area light's direction of emission.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Indirect lighting implementation

Global illumination adds the light that reaches a point after bouncing off other surfaces, which I estimate by tracing a path one random bounce at a time. To pick each bounce direction, DiffuseBSDF::sample_f draws \(\omega_i\) from a cosine-weighted hemisphere sampler, so \(p(\omega_i) = \frac{\cos\theta_i}{\pi}\), and returns \(f = \frac{\rho}{\pi}\). Directions near the normal contribute the most to the reflection equation, so sampling them more often lowers variance.

est_radiance_global_illumination traces the camera ray and returns the environment light (black here) if it misses. If max_ray_depth is 0, it returns only the zero-bounce emission. Otherwise it adds the zero-bounce emission to at_least_one_bounce_radiance. Each camera ray starts with depth = max_ray_depth, and every bounce lowers it by one, so the depth counts how many bounces the path still has left.

at_least_one_bounce_radiance first adds the direct lighting at the hit point from Part 3. It stops once the depth reaches 1. Otherwise it samples a new direction with sample_f, casts a ray from the hit point with depth \(-1\), and, if that ray hits something, calls itself recursively at the new hit point. The returned radiance is weighted the same way as in the direct lighting estimator:

\[ L_{out} \mathrel{+}= \frac{f(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, L_{in}\, \cos\theta_i}{p(\omega_i)\; p_{rr}}. \]

The extra \(p_{rr}\) comes from Russian roulette. After the first bounce, which is always taken, a path continues with probability \(p_{rr} = 0.65\) and is cut off otherwise. Dividing the surviving paths by \(p_{rr}\) keeps the estimate unbiased, because the paths that continue make up for the ones that were cut. Without this, -m 100 would trace 100 bounces for every sample.

The -o flag (isAccumBounces) controls whether bounces are summed. With -o 1, every hit adds its direct lighting, so the image holds all bounces up to the max depth. With -o 0, the zero-bounce term is skipped and only the last hit on the path (depth 1) adds direct lighting, so the image shows only light that bounced exactly max_ray_depth times.

Global illumination results

All of these use 1024 samples per pixel, 1 light ray and a max ray depth of 5.

Direct vs. indirect illumination

Both use CBspheres_lambertian with 1024 samples per pixel and 1 light ray. The direct image uses a max depth of 1. For the indirect image, I built a copy of the renderer whose at_least_one_bounce_radiance skips the direct lighting term at the first hit, so only bounced light remains (max depth 5).

Direct illumination alone looks harsh, because only surfaces that can see the light get any light. The ceiling is black, since the light faces down and no part of the ceiling faces it, and the shadows under the spheres and the bottoms of the spheres are pure black. The indirect image is almost the opposite. It is dim and soft, and it is brightest exactly where the direct image is dark: the ceiling, the shadowed floor and the undersides of the spheres. It also shows color bleeding, since light that bounces off the red and blue walls tints the spheres and the nearby floor. The light itself is black in the indirect image because the emission is zero-bounce light. Adding the two gives the full global illumination image above.

Bounces of light on CBbunny

These use CBbunny with 1024 samples per pixel and 1 light ray. The first row shows only the \(m\)th bounce of light (-o 0), and the second shows all bounces summed up to depth \(m\) (-o 1).

Each bounce is dimmer than the one before. Every diffuse surface absorbs part of the light that hits it, so the energy left after each bounce shrinks by a factor of the albedo, which is why the 4th and 5th bounces are nearly black. The 2nd bounce is the one that changes the image the most. It lights the ceiling, fills in the bunny's shadow and the side of the bunny facing away from the light, and carries red and blue from the walls onto the bunny and the floor. The 3rd bounce looks like a dimmer and blurrier version of the 2nd, because by then the light has scattered off several surfaces and arrives from almost every direction.

In the accumulated images, going from depth 1 to 2 makes the biggest difference, turning the black ceiling and hard shadows into a softly lit room. Depths 3 to 5 only make the scene a little brighter, since each added bounce carries less light. This suggests that the 2nd and 3rd bounces are what make the render look realistic compared to rasterization. A rasterizer only computes direct lighting and fakes the rest with a constant ambient term, which brightens everything equally. The real bounced light is uneven: it is stronger in corners and near bright surfaces, picks up the color of the walls it bounced off, and gives soft contact shadows under the bunny.

Russian roulette

These use CBbunny with 1024 samples per pixel, 1 light ray and Russian roulette, varying the max ray depth.

The images from depth 3 onward look almost the same, because the paths that survive past a few bounces carry very little light. Russian roulette also keeps depth 100 affordable. Each bounce after the first survives with probability 0.65, so a path makes about \(1/(1-0.65) \approx 2.9\) extra bounces on average, and almost none reach depth 100. The depth-100 render took 70 s, about the same as depth 4 (60 s).

Samples per pixel

These use CBspheres_lambertian with 4 light rays and a max depth of 5, varying the samples per pixel (-s).

At 1 sample per pixel, the image is covered in speckled noise. Each pixel follows one random path, so it either reaches the light or it doesn't, and the shadows and ceiling are very grainy. The noise drops as the sample count grows, because the error of a Monte Carlo estimate falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Each 4x increase in samples halves the noise. At 16 samples the image is recognizable but still grainy, at 64 the grain is only visible on the ceiling and in the shadows, and at 1024 the image is clean. Areas lit mostly by indirect light, like the ceiling, stay noisy the longest, since only bounced paths reach them.
