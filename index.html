Overview

In this homework, I built a physics-based path tracer to render realistic lighting. It generates camera rays, intersects them with triangles and spheres, and speeds up intersection with a bounding volume hierarchy. Additionally, it estimates direct lighting with both uniform hemisphere sampling and light sampling, adds global illumination with recursive bounces cut off by Russian roulette, and uses adaptive sampling to stop early on pixels that have converged. For me, the most interesting part was seeing how much of the realism improvements came from variance reduction (making the Monte Carlo estimate less noisy) rather than new physics.

Task 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

For each pixel, raytrace_pixel takes ns_aa random points inside the pixel, generates a camera ray through each one, and averages the radiance they return. generate_ray maps a normalized image point \((x, y) \in [0,1]^2\) onto the sensor plane at \(z = -1\) in camera space,

\[ \mathbf{D}_{cam} = \left((2x-1)\tan\frac{hFov}{2},\; (2y-1)\tan\frac{vFov}{2},\; -1\right), \]

then rotates it into world space with c2w and normalizes it. The ray starts at the camera position.

Each ray is then tested against the scene's primitives. A hit only counts if \(t_{min} \le t \le t_{max}\), and each valid hit sets \(t_{max} = t\), so the closest hit is the one left at the end. Its \(t\), normal, primitive and BSDF are stored in the intersection. For a sphere, substituting the ray into \((\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})^2 = R^2\) gives a quadratic in \(t\), and I take the nearest root in range.

For triangles I used the Möller-Trumbore algorithm. Writing the hit point in barycentric coordinates, \(\mathbf{O} + t\mathbf{D} = (1-b_1-b_2)\mathbf{P}_0 + b_1\mathbf{P}_1 + b_2\mathbf{P}_2\), gives three equations in three unknowns, which solve to

\[ \left[\begin{array}{c} t \\ b_1 \\ b_2 \end{array}\right] = \frac{1}{\mathbf{S}_1 \cdot \mathbf{E}_1} \left[\begin{array}{c} \mathbf{S}_2 \cdot \mathbf{E}_2 \\ \mathbf{S}_1 \cdot \mathbf{S} \\ \mathbf{S}_2 \cdot \mathbf{D} \end{array}\right] \]

Here \(\mathbf{E}_1 = \mathbf{P}_1 - \mathbf{P}_0\) and \(\mathbf{E}_2 = \mathbf{P}_2 - \mathbf{P}_0\) are two edges of the triangle, \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{O} - \mathbf{P}_0\), \(\mathbf{S}_1 = \mathbf{D} \times \mathbf{E}_2\) and \(\mathbf{S}_2 = \mathbf{S} \times \mathbf{E}_1\). The ray hits the triangle when \(b_1 \ge 0\), \(b_2 \ge 0\), \(b_1 + b_2 \le 1\) and \(t\) is in range. The normal at the hit point is the three vertex normals blended with the same barycentric weights.

Task 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

For the BVH, I used construct_bvh to build the tree recursively. Each call computes the bounding box of its primitives and stores it in a new node. If there are at most max_leaf_size primitives, the node is a leaf. Otherwise I split along the axis where the centroids are most spread out, at the average centroid on that axis, reorder the primitives in place with std::partition, and recurse on each half.

The ray-box test treats the box as three pairs of axis-aligned planes: each pair gives an entry and exit time, \(t = (p' - o)/d\), and the ray hits the box if the largest entry time is no later than the smallest exit time. Then, traversal skips any node whose box the ray misses, tests the primitives at a leaf, and visits both children of an interior node, since their boxes can overlap and either may hold the closest hit.

Without a BVH, every ray is tested against every primitive, so render time grows in step with the primitive count: maxplanck has about 20 times as many primitives as the teapot and took about 20 times as long. With the BVH, a ray only goes into boxes it hits, so the cost is about \(O(\log n)\). Every scene rendered in 0.02 to 0.04 s, with only 2 to 6 primitive tests per ray on average. This is why the speedup grows with scene size, from 36× on the teapot to 540× on maxplanck.

Task 3: Direct Illumination

Both direct lighting functions estimate the reflection equation with Monte Carlo,

\[ L_{out}(p, \omega_o) \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_j \to \omega_o)\, L_{in}(p, \omega_j) \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)}, \]

and differ only in how they choose the directions \(\omega_j\). Directions are handled in a local frame where the normal is \(z\), so \(\cos\theta_j\) is the \(z\) component, and every ray leaving a surface starts at \(t_{min} = \epsilon\) so it doesn't hit the surface it left.

Uniform hemisphere sampling draws \(N\) directions uniformly over the hemisphere, so \(p(\omega_j) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\). For each one I cast a ray, and if it hits something, that surface's emission is \(L_{in}\). Light sampling instead calls sample_L on each light, which returns a direction toward a point on the light, its distance, radiance and pdf. I skip samples behind the surface, cast a shadow ray with \(t_{max}\) = distance \(- \epsilon\), and add the sample only if nothing blocks it. Area lights get ns_area_light samples, and point lights get one, since every sample of a point light is the same.

These soft shadow renders use CBbunny with 1 sample per pixel and light sampling.

With 1 light ray, each point in the penumbra is either fully lit or fully dark, depending on whether its single shadow ray reached the light, so the shadow is a little speckle of black and white. More light rays average over more points on the area light, so each point gets closer to the true fraction of the light it can see. At 16 rays the penumbra is a smooth gradient with some grain, and at 64 it is pretty much noise-free.

At the same sample count, light sampling is far less noisy than uniform hemisphere sampling, because it aims every sample at the light instead of wasting most of them on directions that miss it.

The area light covers only a small part of the hemisphere, so with uniform sampling most samples miss it and add nothing, while the few that hit add a large value. That is what puts grain over every surface in the hemisphere images. Light sampling only wastes a sample when the point is actually in shadow, so the walls are smooth and the soft shadows are clean. Both methods converge to the same image, but light sampling needs far fewer samples to get there, and it also works with point lights, which a random hemisphere direction can never hit.

Task 4: Global Illumination

Indirect lighting is estimated by tracing a path with random bounces. est_radiance_global_illumination returns the emission at the first hit plus at_least_one_bounce_radiance, which adds the direct lighting at the hit point. If depth remains, it then samples one bounce direction with DiffuseBSDF::sample_f (cosine-weighted, \(p(\omega_i) = \frac{\cos\theta_i}{\pi}\)), recurses at the point that ray hits, and adds

\[ \frac{f(\omega_i \to \omega_o)\, L_{in}\, \cos\theta_i}{p(\omega_i)\; p_{rr}}. \]

After the first bounce, Russian roulette continues the path with probability \(p_{rr} = 0.65\), and dividing by \(p_{rr}\) keeps the estimate unbiased. With isAccumBounces off, only the last hit adds direct lighting, so the image shows only light that bounced exactly max_ray_depth times.

Global illumination renders use 1024 samples per pixel, 1 light ray and a max depth of 5.

Direct illumination only reaches surfaces that can see the light, so the ceiling, the shadows and the undersides of the spheres are black. Indirect illumination is dim and soft, and it is brightest exactly where the direct image is dark. It also shows color bleeding from the red and blue walls onto the spheres and floor.

These CBbunny renders show only the \(m\)th bounce (isAccumBounces off), at 1024 samples per pixel.


When we isolate each bounce, we can see that they get darker as the depth increases. This is because part of the light is being absorbed each iteration while other light is reflected.

The 2nd bounce lights the ceiling, fills in the bunny's shadow and the side of the bunny facing away from the light, and carries red and blue from the walls onto the bunny and floor. The 3rd bounce is a dimmer, blurrier version of the 2nd, because by then the light arrives from almost every direction. These bounces are what rasterization misses. A rasterizer computes direct light and fakes the rest with a constant ambient term, while real bounced light is uneven: it is stronger in corners, tinted by nearby walls, and gives soft contact shadows.

These CBbunny renders accumulate all bounces up to depth \(m\), at 1024 samples per pixel.

Compared to the single-bounce images, the accumulated images get brighter with depth instead of darker, because each one adds a new bounce on top of the earlier ones. Going from depth 1 to 2 changes the most, turning the black ceiling and hard shadows into a softly lit room, while depths 3 to 5 only brighten the scene slightly, since each extra bounce carries less light.

These CBbunny renders use Russian roulette, at 1024 samples per pixel.

From depth 3 on, the images look almost the same.

These CBspheres_lambertian renders use 4 light rays and a max depth of 5.

At 1 sample per pixel the image is covered in speckled noise, since each pixel follows a single random path. The noise falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so each 4× increase in samples halves it. At 64 samples, grain remains only on the ceiling and in the shadows, which are lit only by bounced light, and at 1024 the image is clean.

Task 5: Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive sampling stops taking samples for a pixel once it has converged, so easy pixels like directly lit walls get few samples and hard pixels like shadows get many. As raytrace_pixel samples, I keep running sums of each sample's illuminance, \(s_1 = \sum x_k\) and \(s_2 = \sum x_k^2\). After every samplesPerBatch samples, I compute

\[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right), \qquad I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

If \(I \le \text{maxTolerance} \cdot \mu\), the pixel's true value is within the tolerance of the mean with 95% confidence, so I stop. Otherwise sampling continues up to ns_aa. The pixel is set to the average of its \(n\) samples, and \(n\) is saved in sampleCountBuffer for the sample rate image.

Both renders use 2048 max samples per pixel, batches of 64, a tolerance of 0.05, 1 light ray and a max depth of 5. Red means many samples and blue means few.

Walls facing the light and the light itself converge quickly and are blue. The ceiling and the shadows under the bunny and spheres are red, because they are lit only by bounced paths, few of which reach the light.

I found it interesting that adaptive sampling gives basically the same image as taking all 2048 samples at every pixel, even though the blue areas stop a long time before that.
